The idea

Norwegian childhood stories become a Pacific question.

Håkon opens with Thor Heyerdahl's old gravity: the tales he grew up with, the call for continued exploration, and a new ambition to sail balsawood rafts to Easter Island and back.

“Being Norwegian, I grew up with tales of Thor Heyerdahl's brave Kon-Tiki expedition.”
Original illustrated story Balsa arrives
02

The build

A raft assembled like a rule set: flexible, visible, tied together.

Volunteers tied the logs with natural fibre rope. No nails, no screws, just disciplined structure and trust in materials. Underneath, echo sounders joined an ancient silhouette to modern instruments.

“Volunteers quickly went to work and tied the logs together with natural fibre rope — no nails or screws!”
Rope and logs Ready to lift
03

The launch

Flags, blessings, ceremonies, then the open sea.

The Peruvian Navy opened the shipyard and sent the rafts out with ceremony. The story moves from dockside dignity to a simple operational truth: before leaving, there was always one more thing to finish.

“The Peruvian Navy generously allowed us to use their shipyard, and held a noble ceremony when the two rafts were launched into the ocean.”
Ceremony Departure
04

The crossing

The ocean teaches the interface.

Two rafts, seven crew members each, left Callao on November 7. Steering came through guara boards. Feedback came as water over the deck, waves into the cabin, and the constant need to adjust.

“We quickly learnt how to adjust guara boards up or down to set a course.”
Under sail Wet deck
05

The science

A raft can still ask twenty-first-century questions.

The expedition looked for plastics, launched drifters, lowered a CTD to 2,046 metres, and used a manta trawl when weather allowed. The old form carried new instruments.

“At sea, we looked for plastics, released drifters, and sent a CTD down to 2046 metres — and winched it back up!”
CTD work Manta trawl
06

Life onboard

Food, birds, whales, weather, and a bottle standing still.

The story keeps its humor because the details are so human: oatmeal, beans, bread, fish, frigatebirds, flying fish, gale-force winds, and an apple cider bottle steady enough to become a tiny miracle.

“We had steady winds all along, often gale force. But the rafts were stable.”
Wildlife Stable raft
07

The electrons

Even at sea, someone has to keep the stack online.

Håkon's dry line about responsibility for electrons turns the expedition into something wonderfully specific: this was seamanship, science, communication, and debugging in the Pacific.

“I was responsible for all electrons onboard the raft, and most of them behaved.”
Electrons Signals
08

Landfall

Easter Island appears first as a shadow, then as arrival.

After 43 days, the island came into view. Håkon's sentence lands with the whole weight of the page behind it: not just arrival, but an honorable way of arriving.

“This is an honorable way of arriving on Easter Island. Epic.”
Land ahead Grass again
